2. How can we measure health inequality?

24 September 2026

How can we turn the idea of health inequality into something we can actually measure? In this session, you will learn how to define health outcomes and dimensions of inequality, distinguish between absolute and relative measures, and conduct your first empirical analysis of health inequality in Switzerland using survey data.

About this session

This is a guided self-study session. There is no regular classroom meeting. Work through this page in the order presented and complete the exercises as you go.

The session combines three elements:

  1. Concepts — what exactly do we mean when we measure health inequality?
  2. Data — how can health and social position be measured using survey data?
  3. Analysis — how can we describe and quantify health inequalities in R?

We will use Switzerland as our laboratory. The next session will extend the analysis to comparisons across countries and over time.

NoteSoftware used in this course

The empirical exercises are written in R and can be completed in RStudio. No prior experience with R is required: the exercises introduce the commands needed step by step.

If you prefer to work in Stata, equivalent support and code can be provided on request.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  • distinguish between a health outcome, a dimension of inequality and a measure of inequality;
  • identify relevant health and socioeconomic variables in survey data;
  • calculate health outcomes for different population groups;
  • distinguish between absolute and relative health inequalities;
  • recognise the difference between a gap and a gradient;
  • import, inspect, recode and summarise survey data in R; and
  • interpret a simple empirical analysis of health inequality in Switzerland.

From a concept to a measure

In Session 1, we defined health inequality as:

Health inequality refers to systematic differences in health outcomes between individuals or social groups.

This definition is deliberately broad. Before we can study health inequality empirically, we have to make several choices.

Consider the following hypothetical result:

Among people with tertiary education, 90% report good or very good health. Among people with compulsory education, the corresponding figure is 75%.

There is clearly a difference between the two groups. But describing this as a health inequality requires us to specify three things.

Three building blocks of measuring health inequality

Health outcome × dimension of inequality × measure of inequality

For example:

Self-rated health × education × absolute difference

The first question is what aspect of health we want to measure. The second is between whom health is being compared. The third is how we quantify the difference.

These choices matter. Different measures can describe different aspects of the same distribution of health.

1. Choosing a health outcome

Health is multidimensional. No single variable captures a person’s complete health status.

Population surveys therefore use different indicators, for example:

  • self-rated health;
  • chronic illness;
  • limitations in everyday activities;
  • mental health and depressive symptoms;
  • disability;
  • healthcare use;
  • health behaviours.

In this session, we begin with one of the most widely used indicators in population health research: self-rated health.

Respondents to the European Social Survey (ESS), for example, are asked to assess their health in general on a five-category scale ranging from very good to very bad. In the existing ESS data workflow, this variable is available as health. :contentReferenceoaicite:0

There are different ways to analyse such a variable. We could retain all five categories, assign them numerical values, or distinguish respondents who report good or very good health from everyone else.

For our first analysis, we will use the latter approach.

TipA measurement choice is an analytical choice

Recoding a five-category health measure into a binary variable makes the results easy to communicate:

“82% of respondents report good or very good health.”

But information is lost when categories are combined. There is therefore no universally correct recoding. Your choice should be substantively justified and reported clearly.

2. Choosing a dimension of inequality

Next, we need to decide between whom health is being compared.

Health inequalities can be studied across many dimensions, including:

  • education;
  • income;
  • occupation or social class;
  • gender;
  • migration background or ethnicity;
  • age;
  • geographic location.

During the semester, we will examine several of these dimensions in detail.

For now, we focus on education. Education is particularly useful for learning how to measure socioeconomic health inequalities because it provides an ordered indicator of socioeconomic position.

A simple analysis might compare people with relatively low and high levels of education. A more complete analysis can examine whether health improves progressively as educational attainment increases.

This distinction leads us to two related ideas.

Health gaps and health gradients

A health gap compares particular groups, often two ends of a social distribution.

For example:

What is the difference in good self-rated health between people with low and high education?

A social gradient in health considers the entire social hierarchy.

Rather than simply comparing the lowest and highest groups, we ask whether health tends to improve progressively with socioeconomic position.

Note

A large part of health inequality research therefore concerns not only whether two groups differ, but also how health is distributed across the full socioeconomic hierarchy.

3. Choosing a measure of inequality

Return to our hypothetical example:

Education Good or very good health
Compulsory education 75%
Tertiary education 90%

How large is the inequality?

There is more than one reasonable answer.

Absolute inequality

An absolute difference expresses the difference in the original units of the outcome.

For our example:

[ 90% - 75% = 15 ]

The absolute health inequality is therefore 15 percentage points.

More generally, the rate difference (RD) between two groups can be written as:

[ RD = y_1-y_2 ]

where (y_1) and (y_2) are the health outcomes of the two groups being compared.

Relative inequality

A relative measure expresses the outcome of one group relative to the outcome of another.

Using the same example:

[ =1.20 ]

The prevalence of good health is therefore 1.20 times as high among the high-education group as among the low-education group.

The corresponding rate ratio (RR) is:

[ RR= ]

WarningPercentage or percentage point?

These are not the same.

An increase from 75% to 90% is an increase of 15 percentage points.

Relative to the initial value of 75%, it is an increase of 20%.

Always state clearly which quantity you are reporting.

Absolute and relative inequality can tell different stories

Why do we need both measures?

Consider two equally sized population groups whose life expectancy initially differs:

Group Life expectancy
Group A 20 years
Group B 30 years

Now imagine two different interventions.

With one intervention, the same number of years is added to the life expectancy of both groups. With another, life expectancy in both groups is increased by the same proportion.

Both interventions could improve average population health substantially. But they need not have the same consequences for measured health inequality.

ImportantThink before continuing

If both groups gain the same number of years, what happens to:

  1. the absolute difference between them?
  2. the relative ratio between them?

What if both groups instead experience the same proportional increase?

The important point is not that one measure is inherently superior to the other.

It is that absolute and relative measures capture different properties of a distribution. An absolute inequality can decrease while a relative inequality increases, or vice versa.

For this reason, good empirical practice is often to report both absolute and relative measures, together with the underlying health levels of the groups being compared.

From concepts to data

We will now apply these ideas to real survey data.

Throughout the empirical part of the course, we will primarily work with two major international survey programmes:

European Social Survey (ESS)

The European Social Survey is a cross-national survey conducted across European countries. It contains information on respondents’ social, demographic and economic characteristics as well as attitudes, behaviours and several health indicators.

Some rounds contain particularly extensive health modules.

For our purposes, useful variables include measures of:

  • self-rated health;
  • limitations in daily activities;
  • depressive symptoms;
  • health behaviours;
  • education;
  • household income;
  • age and gender;
  • place of residence;
  • social relationships.

International Social Survey Programme (ISSP)

The International Social Survey Programme conducts recurring thematic survey modules across a large number of countries.

The Health and Health Care modules from 2011 and 2021 are particularly useful for this course. They combine health measures with information about healthcare, socioeconomic position and respondents’ social and demographic characteristics.

The ISSP also harmonises a range of background variables across participating countries, although some variables originate from country-specific measures and require care when making international comparisons. :contentReferenceoaicite:1

We will return to this issue in Session 3.

Your first analysis in R

We now turn to the empirical part of the session.

Our first question is deliberately simple:

How does self-rated health vary by education in Switzerland?

We will first answer this question using the European Social Survey.

The workflow is:

Import → inspect → select Switzerland → inspect variables → recode → describe → compare → visualise → interpret

Do not worry if some of the R syntax is unfamiliar. The aim is not to memorise commands. Focus first on understanding what each step does and why we need it.

Step 1: Prepare RStudio

Open RStudio and create a new R script.

At the top of the script, load the packages we need:

library(haven)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
library(scales)

Packages extend the functionality of R. Here:

  • haven allows us to import Stata data files;
  • dplyr provides convenient tools for manipulating data;
  • ggplot2 is used to create graphs; and
  • scales helps format quantities such as percentages.

If R tells you that a package is not installed, install it once using, for example:

install.packages("haven")

You do not need to run install.packages() every time you use R.

Step 2: Import the ESS data

Download the ESS dataset provided for this session and save it in your working folder.

Then import it:

ess11 <- read_dta("../../data/ESS11.dta")

The object ess11 now contains the survey data.

You can inspect its basic structure:

dim(ess11)
names(ess11)

dim() tells you how many rows and columns the dataset contains. In survey data, a row usually represents one respondent and a column represents one variable.

You can also inspect the first few observations:

head(ess11)
TipDo not try to understand every variable

Large survey datasets can contain hundreds of variables. In applied research, you normally identify the variables relevant to your research question using the questionnaire and codebook, and then inspect those variables carefully.

Step 3: Select Switzerland

The ESS contains respondents from several countries. The country identifier is cntry.

First inspect the countries included in the data:

table(ess11$cntry)

We now create a dataset containing only respondents from Switzerland:

ch <- ess11 %>%
  filter(cntry == "CH")

Check the number of observations:

nrow(ch)

From this point onwards, ch is our Swiss analytical dataset.

Note

The %>% symbol is called the pipe. It passes the result of one operation to the next operation. You can read

ess11 %>% filter(cntry == "CH")

roughly as:

“Take ess11, then keep observations for which the country is Switzerland.”

Step 4: Inspect self-rated health

The ESS variable health measures respondents’ general self-rated health.

Before transforming any variable, always inspect its coding.

table(ch$health, useNA = "ifany")

Because the dataset was imported using haven, the variable also contains value labels. You can inspect them using:

attr(ch$health, "labels")

The categories run from very good to very bad health.

For our first analysis, we create a binary outcome that equals 1 if a respondent reports good or very good health, and 0 otherwise.

ch <- ch %>%
  mutate(
    health_good = case_when(
      health %in% c(1, 2) ~ 1,
      health %in% c(3, 4, 5) ~ 0,
      TRUE ~ NA_real_
    )
  )

Check the new variable:

table(ch$health_good, useNA = "ifany")

And calculate the proportion of Swiss respondents reporting good or very good health:

mean(ch$health_good, na.rm = TRUE)

Because health_good is coded 0/1, its mean is the proportion of respondents coded 1.

Multiplying by 100 gives a percentage:

mean(ch$health_good, na.rm = TRUE) * 100
ImportantCheck your understanding

What does the number you have just calculated mean?

Write one complete sentence reporting the result before continuing.

Step 5: Inspect education

The ESS provides a harmonised education variable called eisced.

Inspect it:

table(ch$eisced, useNA = "ifany")
attr(ch$eisced, "labels")

Before comparing health across educational groups, make sure you understand what the categories represent.

This is an important general principle:

Never analyse a survey variable merely because you know its variable name.

Always check its question wording, coding, missing values and substantive meaning first.

Once created, inspect it:

Step 6: Calculate health by education

We can now calculate the proportion reporting good health separately for each educational group:

health_by_education <- ch %>%
  group_by(education) %>%
  summarise(
    good_health = mean(health_good, na.rm = TRUE),
    n = sum(!is.na(health_good))
  )

Display the result:

health_by_education

The output contains two important pieces of information:

  • good_health tells us the proportion reporting good or very good health;
  • n tells us how many respondents contribute to the estimate.

For easier interpretation, we can express the outcome as a percentage:

health_by_education <- health_by_education %>%
  mutate(good_health_pct = good_health * 100)

health_by_education
ImportantExercise 1: Describe the pattern

Look at the results and answer the following questions.

  1. Which educational group reports the highest level of good health?
  2. Which reports the lowest?
  3. How large is the difference in percentage points?
  4. Does the pattern suggest only a gap between the extremes, or also a broader educational gradient?
  5. What can you conclude from these descriptive results — and what can you not conclude?

The last question is especially important. These results describe an association between education and health. They do not establish that education itself caused the observed differences.

Step 7: Calculate an absolute health inequality

Suppose the percentage reporting good health is (y_H) in the highest education group and (y_L) in the lowest education group.

The absolute difference is:

[ RD=y_H-y_L ]

You can calculate this directly from your results.

high <- health_by_education %>%
  filter(education == "High") %>%
  pull(good_health)

low <- health_by_education %>%
  filter(education == "Low") %>%
  pull(good_health)

rate_difference <- (high - low) * 100

rate_difference

Interpret the result in percentage points.

Step 8: Calculate a relative health inequality

Using the same groups, calculate:

[ RR= ]

In R:

rate_ratio <- high / low

rate_ratio

A rate ratio of 1 would indicate equal levels of good health in the two groups. A value above 1 indicates that good health is more common in the high-education group.

ImportantExercise 2: Absolute and relative inequality

Report your two results in words:

The absolute difference in good self-rated health between … is … percentage points.

The prevalence of good self-rated health is … times as high among … as among …

Which measure do you find easier to interpret? What information would be lost if you reported only one of them?

Step 9: Visualise the inequality

A graph often communicates a health inequality more effectively than a table.

ggplot(
  health_by_education,
  aes(x = education, y = good_health)
) +
  geom_col() +
  scale_y_continuous(
    labels = percent_format(),
    limits = c(0, 1)
  ) +
  labs(
    x = "Education",
    y = "Good or very good health",
    title = "Self-rated health by education in Switzerland",
    subtitle = "European Social Survey"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Look carefully at the graph.

Does it communicate the same pattern that you identified in the table?

What does the graph make easier to see? What information from the table is no longer visible?

A second dataset: can we reproduce the pattern?

So far, we have measured educational health inequality using the ESS.

We now repeat the basic logic using a different survey: the ISSP 2021 Health and Health Care module.

This is useful for two reasons.

First, it familiarises you with a second dataset that you may use for your empirical project.

Second, it illustrates an important feature of empirical research: substantively similar concepts often have different variable names and coding schemes across datasets.

In ISSP 2021, the existing course workflow uses:

  • v51 for self-rated health;
  • EDULEVEL for education; and
  • WEIGHT_COM as the combined survey weight. :contentReferenceoaicite:2

Your task is therefore not simply to copy the ESS commands. You first need to understand how the corresponding ISSP variables are coded.

Beyond education and self-rated health

You now know the basic logic for measuring one form of health inequality.

But the same workflow can be applied to many other questions.

The existing ESS data, for example, also contain measures related to limitations in daily activities, depressive symptoms, alcohol consumption, income, urban/rural residence and social contact. :contentReferenceoaicite:3

The ISSP health modules contain additional health and healthcare variables and also provide a 10-point measure of respondents’ subjective position in the social hierarchy (TOPBOT). :contentReferenceoaicite:4

This creates many possible combinations:

Health outcome Dimension of inequality Possible comparison
Self-rated health Education Low vs high / gradient
Self-rated health Income Lower vs higher income
Depressive symptoms Education Educational gradient
Activity limitation Social position Lower vs higher position
Self-rated health Place Urban vs rural

The analytical logic remains the same:

Define → measure → compare → quantify → visualise → interpret

Independent exercise

Choose one additional health-inequality question using either ESS or ISSP data for Switzerland.

For example:

Is poor mental health more common among people with lower socioeconomic position?

or:

Does self-rated health differ between urban and rural residents?

Your analysis should contain:

  1. a clearly formulated descriptive research question;
  2. one health outcome;
  3. one dimension of inequality;
  4. a description of how both variables are measured;
  5. the health outcome for the relevant groups;
  6. at least one measure of inequality;
  7. one graph; and
  8. a short interpretation of the result.
TipKeep the question descriptive

At this stage, formulate questions such as:

How does health vary by education?

rather than:

What is the effect of education on health?

The first asks about an observed health inequality. The second makes a causal claim and requires a different research design.

What have we learned?

Measuring health inequality requires more than calculating a difference between two numbers.

Every analysis involves choices about:

  1. what health outcome to study;
  2. which dimension of inequality to examine;
  3. how the relevant variables are operationalised;
  4. whether inequality is measured in absolute or relative terms; and
  5. whether we are interested in a gap between groups or a gradient across a social hierarchy.

The same population can appear more or less unequal depending on these choices. This is not necessarily a statistical problem: different measures capture different aspects of health inequality.

Good empirical analysis therefore makes these choices explicit and reports enough information for readers to understand what has been measured.

The basic empirical workflow

Research question → health outcome → dimension of inequality → variables → group-specific health levels → absolute and relative inequality → visualisation → interpretation
