11. Which policies successfully reduce health inequalities?

26 November 2026

Can governments reduce health inequalities—and what does it take to do so? After examining the patterns, explanations and consequences of health inequalities, we turn to policies that have made a difference. We compare four historical episodes of declining health inequalities, consider the political conditions that made reform possible, and ask whether effective policies also enjoy public support.

Today’s questions: Which policies work? Why is reducing health inequalities difficult? How do public attitudes and politics shape policy responses?

Learning objectives: Compare policy approaches and historical cases; identify four mechanisms for reducing inequalities; distinguish absolute and relative gaps; and explain political barriers to effective policy.

1. From explaining inequalities to reducing them

We have examined the patterns and causes of health inequalities. We now turn to what governments can do to reduce them.

Three policy goals are worth distinguishing:

  • Improve population health overall.
  • Improve health among disadvantaged groups.
  • Reduce health inequalities between groups.

These goals are not identical. Consider a hypothetical reduction in mortality rates (per 100,000 people):

Socioeconomic group Before After
Lower socioeconomic position 1,000 600
Higher socioeconomic position 500 250

Both groups benefit, but inequality changes differently depending on how it is measured:

  • Absolute gap: 500 → 350 (decreases).
  • Relative ratio: 2.0 → 2.4 (increases).

Mackenbach argues that reducing absolute inequalities is often a more realistic policy goal, even when relative inequalities persist (Mackenbach 2019, ch. 6; Mackenbach 2020).

Why is reducing health inequality so difficult?

Health inequalities are often described as a “wicked problem”, with interacting causes and no simple solution. Key challenges include:

  • Complex causes: Social, economic and behavioural factors interact.
  • Reverse causality: Poor health can itself cause socioeconomic disadvantage.
  • Lifestyle drift: Structural policy ambitions often become individual behaviour interventions.
  • Political constraints: Evidence alone does not guarantee policy action or sustained implementation.

Reducing health inequalities therefore requires coordinated, large-scale and politically sustained policies (Bambra et al. 2025). Moreover, evidence about the causes of health inequalities does not necessarily establish which policies will reduce them (Mackenbach 2019, ch. 6).

Looking upstream: The policy determinants of health

The political economy perspective asks not only why particular risk factors are unequally distributed, but also which policies produce or change that distribution (Bambra et al. 2025, ch. 1).

2. Four historical episodes of narrowing health inequalities

Rather than reviewing only small interventions, Bambra et al. (2025) look back at periods in which health gaps actually narrowed. Their four country cases differ in institutions, period and policy mix. This makes comparison informative, but also limits simple causal attribution.

United States: The Great Society (1960s)

  • Policy package: anti-poverty programmes, food assistance, educational investment, Medicare and Medicaid, desegregation and civil rights legislation, and expanded political rights.
  • Observed change: reductions in racial and income-related gaps in infant and premature mortality from the mid-1960s into the 1970s.
  • Why it mattered: health-relevant living conditions and access to public services changed alongside the political inclusion of groups previously excluded or discriminated against.
  • Qualification: the reforms occurred together, and evidence for the independent contribution of expanded medical insurance is more limited than for some other parts of the package.

Brazil: Democratisation and social reform (1980s–2000s)

  • Policy package: a constitutional right to health and expansion of the public health system (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS), the Family Health Programme, cash transfers, minimum-wage increases and new opportunities for political participation.
  • Observed change: substantial reductions in infant mortality and in geographical and socioeconomic inequalities in childhood health.
  • Why it mattered: healthcare improvements were combined with policies tackling poverty and exclusion, with larger potential gains for previously underserved populations.
  • Qualification: many programmes expanded together, and the precise contribution of each to declining mortality is difficult to identify.

Germany: Reunification (from 1990)

  • Policy package: the incorporation of eastern Germany into western social insurance, pensions, healthcare, infrastructure and other institutions, alongside improvements in living conditions.
  • Observed change: the East–West life-expectancy gap narrowed sharply; the gap for women had largely disappeared by around 2010, while the gap for men remained smaller but persisted.
  • Why it mattered: major institutional and material changes were introduced over a relatively short period.
  • Qualification: reunification is a particularly large policy shock, but a bundle of changes—not a clean experiment isolating the effect of pensions or healthcare alone.

England: National Health Inequalities Strategy (1997–2010)

  • Policy package: explicit inequality targets, additional NHS and primary-care investment, early-years and family support, poverty reduction, and programmes targeted at disadvantaged areas.
  • Observed change: some local and cause-specific inequalities improved during the strategy, and later analyses found more favourable trends in certain geographic mortality gaps. But the overall national targets for narrowing life-expectancy and infant-mortality gaps were not achieved.
  • Why it mattered: health inequality reduction became a national political objective linked to explicit targets, funding and coordinated action across sectors.
  • Qualification: Mackenbach (2019) (ch. 6) regards the national result as largely disappointing, despite implementation of many planned measures. He points to weak evidence for some interventions, inappropriate policy entry points and insufficient population-wide reach. Bambra et al. (2025) emphasise the more favourable developments in particular outcomes and places. The distinction illustrates why outcome, measure, period and comparison group matter when evaluating success.

3. The four levellers of health inequalities

Across the cases, Bambra et al. (2025) identify four recurring mechanisms. They are not four mutually exclusive policies; rather, they describe routes through which different policy packages can make health outcomes more equal.

Leveller Typical instruments How it may reduce health inequalities
1. Welfare-state expansion Income support, pensions, child benefits, education and housing investment Improves access to resources and reduces exposure to deprivation.
2. Greater economic equality Minimum wages, labour protections, collective bargaining, redistribution Narrows differences in earnings, security and working conditions.
3. Improved healthcare access Universal coverage, affordable care, primary care and service investment Reduces unequal barriers to effective prevention and treatment.
4. Political incorporation Voting rights, representation, trade unions, participatory institutions Gives disadvantaged groups greater voice and influence over collective decisions.
ImportantWhy healthcare alone is not enough

Better medical access can reduce treatable health gaps. But many inequalities arise before someone reaches the healthcare system—in education, income, working conditions and living environments. The historical cases suggest that combining policies is often more promising than relying on a single sector (Bambra et al. 2025, ch. 6).

Are policies stronger together?

The mechanisms can reinforce one another. Income security may help people benefit from healthcare, while political incorporation can make social programmes more responsive and durable. However, the cases do not identify a universally optimal policy mix.

Policy success can be temporary

The four cases share another feature: none eliminated health inequalities, and their gains were not fully sustained. Reforms can be reversed, eroded by underfunding or offset by economic and political change. This is a reason to distinguish adoption, implementation, effectiveness and durability (Bambra et al. 2025, chs. 6–7).

4. A critical perspective: Why have health inequalities persisted?

The four historical cases show that policy can matter, but Mackenbach (2020) challenges some optimistic assumptions about how readily socioeconomic health gaps can be reduced. His argument rests on three connected points.

1. Causes of inequality are not identical to effective policy levers

Socioeconomic position and health are strongly associated, but the relationship may reflect a combination of social causation, reverse causation and selection or confounding. Mackenbach argues that some causal claims, particularly about income and education, have been harder to establish using rigorous designs than is often assumed. This does not mean that material conditions are irrelevant. It means that policies to reduce disadvantage cannot be assumed automatically to remove every mechanism producing health inequality (Mackenbach 2019, chs. 2, 6; Mackenbach 2020).

2. The welfare-state paradox

Some of Europe’s most generous welfare states have large relative socioeconomic inequalities in mortality. Mackenbach highlights the role of inequalities in smoking, alcohol-related harm and other risk factors, which redistribution alone has not eliminated. This calls for both structural policies and effective, equitable prevention and healthcare—without treating health behaviours as merely unrestricted individual choices (Mackenbach 2019, ch. 6; Mackenbach 2020).

3. Interventions must reach enough people to change population outcomes

A programme can be beneficial for participants yet too small to shift national inequality measures. The English experience illustrates the distance between adopting a strategy, implementing programmes, improving individual outcomes and reducing population-level gaps. Mackenbach therefore recommends realistic targets, careful evaluation and greater attention to absolute inequality, especially when population health is improving (Mackenbach 2019, ch. 6).

NoteComplementary or competing lessons?

Bambra, Lynch and Smith: Historical episodes of narrower health gaps show that political choices and combinations of social, healthcare and political reforms can make a difference.

Mackenbach: The results of deliberate inequality-reduction policies have often been disappointing. We should distinguish causal mechanisms, measure outcomes carefully, and avoid promising reductions that the available evidence and feasible scale cannot support.

These perspectives are not mutually exclusive: the empirical question is which policy, for which population, on which outcome, over what time horizon, and relative to which counterfactual?

5. Learning across countries?

The historical cases provide evidence that large-scale reform can reduce health inequalities. But transferring policies requires more than copying an intervention from one country to another.

For your comparison, examine five questions:

  1. Problem and target: Which inequality is the policy intended to reduce—income, education, racial or regional differences, and which health outcomes?
  2. Policy mechanism: Which of the four levellers does it use? Through what causal pathway should it improve the distribution of health?
  3. Evidence: Is success established by trends, before–after comparisons, quasi-experiments or combinations of evidence? What alternative explanations remain?
  4. Institutions: Which level of government has authority and resources to act? How do political coalitions and administrative capacity matter?
  5. Public support and durability: Who benefits, who bears costs, who opposes reform, and what would make the policy sustainable?

6. Key takeaways

  • Policies can reduce health inequalities, but attributing effects to individual interventions is difficult.
  • Four mechanisms matter: social protection, economic equality, healthcare access and political inclusion.
  • Measurement matters: absolute and relative inequalities can move in opposite directions.
  • Evidence matters: identifying causes does not establish which policies will effectively reduce inequalities.
  • Politics matters: successful policies require public support, institutional capacity and sustained implementation.

Final discussion: Which structural policy should Switzerland prioritise to reduce health inequalities over the next decade? How would you evaluate its success?
