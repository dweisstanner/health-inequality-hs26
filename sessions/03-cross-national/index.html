3. Health inequality across countries

1 October 2026

We have learned how to measure a health inequality within one country. But is an inequality observed in Switzerland large or small? To answer that question, we need comparison. In this session, you will examine how health levels and health inequalities vary across countries and over time using the European Social Survey (ESS) and the International Social Survey Programme (ISSP).

About this session

This is a guided self-study session. There is no regular classroom meeting. Work through this page in the order presented and complete the exercises as you go.

The session builds directly on Session 2.

In Session 2, our basic question was:

How does health vary by education in Switzerland?

We now extend this in two directions:

  1. Across countries: Is the educational health inequality observed in Switzerland larger or smaller than in other countries?
  2. Across time: Has the magnitude of health inequality changed?

The empirical logic remains familiar:

Define → measure → compare → quantify → visualise → interpret

But comparison introduces an additional challenge:

Are we actually comparing the same thing?

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  • compare health levels across countries;
  • compare the magnitude of health inequalities across countries;
  • distinguish between population health and health inequality;
  • compare health inequalities across survey waves;
  • recognise basic problems of cross-national and over-time comparability;
  • use grouped summaries in R to construct country-level estimates;
  • visualise variation in health inequalities across countries; and
  • formulate a comparative empirical question suitable for your own project.

From a national estimate to a comparative question

Suppose we find that:

80% of highly educated respondents in Switzerland report good or very good health, compared with 70% among respondents with low education.

The absolute educational health inequality is:

\[ 80\%-70\%=10 \text{ percentage points} \]

Is 10 percentage points a large inequality?

By itself, the number does not tell us.

If comparable inequalities are only 2 or 3 percentage points in most other countries, Switzerland would appear relatively unequal.

If gaps of 15 or 20 percentage points are common elsewhere, the same Swiss estimate would appear comparatively small.

This illustrates one of the main purposes of comparative research:

Comparison provides a benchmark.

It allows us to ask whether an observed pattern is common, unusual, stable or changing.

Three comparative questions

Comparative health-inequality research often asks three different questions.

1. Do countries differ in average health?

For example:

Is good self-rated health more common in Switzerland than in Germany?

This concerns population health levels.

2. Do countries differ in health inequality?

For example:

Is the educational gap in self-rated health larger in Switzerland than in Germany?

This concerns the distribution of health within countries.

3. Do health inequalities change over time?

For example:

Has the educational health gap in Switzerland become larger or smaller over the past decade?

This concerns change in inequality.

These questions should not be confused.

ImportantA healthy country is not necessarily an equal country

A country can have:

  • high average health and large health inequalities;
  • high average health and small health inequalities;
  • low average health and large health inequalities; or
  • low average health and small health inequalities.

Comparing average health and comparing health inequality are therefore different analytical tasks.

A simple example

Consider two hypothetical countries:

Country Low education High education Absolute gap
Country A 70% 85% 15 pp
Country B 80% 90% 10 pp

Country B has higher levels of good health in both groups.

It also has a smaller absolute educational health inequality.

But now consider:

Country Low education High education Absolute gap
Country C 60% 70% 10 pp
Country D 80% 90% 10 pp

Countries C and D have identical absolute inequalities, but very different levels of population health.

This is why comparative analysis should usually report:

  1. the health level of each group;
  2. the measure of inequality; and
  3. the country context in which the inequality occurs.

Comparability is not automatic

Before comparing countries, we need to ask whether our measures mean approximately the same thing in each case.

Several problems can arise.

Question wording

The wording of a health question may differ between survey programmes.

Even small wording differences can affect responses.

Response categories

Two surveys may both measure self-rated health but offer different response categories.

A binary indicator such as “good health” may therefore not be constructed in exactly the same way.

Education

National education systems differ considerably.

International surveys therefore use harmonised classifications to make educational attainment more comparable across countries.

Even these harmonised measures are approximations.

Survey composition

Countries can differ in:

  • age structure;
  • education levels;
  • migration;
  • labour-market participation;
  • urbanisation; and
  • many other characteristics.

A raw difference between countries may therefore reflect differences in who was surveyed as well as differences in the relationship we are interested in.

Time

Comparisons across survey waves require additional care.

Question wording, coding, survey modes, participating countries and weighting procedures can change.

NoteThe practical rule

For this session, we will compare observations within the same survey programme wherever possible:

  • ESS with ESS;
  • ISSP with ISSP.

We will not treat ESS and ISSP estimates as if they formed one seamless time series.

Part I: Comparing countries using the ESS

We begin with the European Social Survey.

Our first comparative research question is:

How does educational inequality in self-rated health vary across European countries?

We will use ESS Round 11.

The basic strategy is:

Calculate health by education within each country → calculate the educational health gap within each country → compare those gaps

The ESS uses two-letter country codes.

Step 4: Construct the health outcome

We again use self-rated health.

First inspect the original variable:

table(ess11$health, useNA = "ifany")

Create the binary indicator used in Session 2:

ess11 <- ess11 %>%
  mutate(
    health_good = case_when(
      health %in% c(1, 2) ~ 1,
      health %in% c(3, 4, 5) ~ 0,
      TRUE ~ NA_real_
    )
  )

Check the new variable:

table(ess11$health_good, useNA = "ifany")

Step 5: Construct comparable education groups

We use the harmonised ESS education variable eisced.

Inspect it first:

table(ess11$eisced, useNA = "ifany")

For this exercise, we collapse the harmonised categories into three broad groups.

ess11 <- ess11 %>%
  mutate(
    education = case_when(
      eisced %in% c(1, 2) ~ "Low",
      eisced %in% c(3, 4, 5) ~ "Medium",
      eisced %in% c(6, 7) ~ "High",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    )
  )

Make the ordering explicit:

ess11 <- ess11 %>%
  mutate(
    education = factor(
      education,
      levels = c("Low", "Medium", "High")
    )
  )

Inspect the distribution:

table(ess11$education, useNA = "ifany")
NoteWhy simplify education?

The harmonised ESS education variable contains more detail than we need for this exercise.

Collapsing it into low, medium and high education makes the comparative pattern easier to see.

This is a substantive analytical choice rather than a property of the data. In independent research, you should always document how such groups were constructed.

Step 9: Calculate the absolute educational health gap

Graphs are useful, but we also want a numerical measure of inequality.

For each country, extract the health level of the low- and high-education groups.

One convenient approach is to reshape the data.

First create separate low- and high-education datasets:

health_low <- health_by_country_education %>%
  filter(education == "Low") %>%
  select(
    country,
    health_low = good_health
  )
health_high <- health_by_country_education %>%
  filter(education == "High") %>%
  select(
    country,
    health_high = good_health
  )

Combine them:

country_gaps <- health_low %>%
  inner_join(
    health_high,
    by = "country"
  )

Now calculate the absolute difference:

country_gaps <- country_gaps %>%
  mutate(
    absolute_gap = health_high - health_low
  )

Inspect the result:

country_gaps %>%
  arrange(desc(absolute_gap))

To express the difference in percentage points:

country_gaps <- country_gaps %>%
  mutate(
    absolute_gap_pp = absolute_gap * 100
  )

Now the interpretation is straightforward.

For example:

The proportion reporting good health is 12 percentage points higher among respondents with high education than among respondents with low education.

ggplot(

  coord_flip() +
  scale_y_continuous(
    labels = percent_format()
  ) +
  labs(

The first graph showed:

Step 11: Calculate relative inequalities

As in Session 2, we should not rely only on absolute differences.

Calculate the relative ratio:

country_gaps <- country_gaps %>%
  mutate(
    relative_ratio = health_high / health_low
  )

Inspect:

country_gaps %>%
  arrange(desc(relative_ratio))

A value of 1 indicates no relative difference.

A value of 1.20, for example, means that the prevalence of good health is 1.20 times as high in the high-education group as in the low-education group.

Now compare the country ranking based on:

  • absolute_gap; and
  • relative_ratio.
country_gaps %>%
  select(
    country,
    health_low,
    health_high,
    absolute_gap_pp,
    relative_ratio
  ) %>%
  arrange(desc(absolute_gap_pp))
ImportantExercise 4: Does the measure matter?

Do countries receive exactly the same ranking when you use:

  • the absolute difference, and
  • the relative ratio?

If not, why might the rankings differ?

Which measure would you report in a research paper?

A strong answer is usually:

Both, together with the underlying group-specific health levels.

Part II: Comparing health inequalities over time

Cross-national comparison gives us one benchmark.

A second benchmark is time.

Suppose Switzerland has an educational health gap of 10 percentage points today.

Was the gap smaller in the past?

Larger?

Or broadly unchanged?

We can examine this by comparing different rounds of the same survey.

For the ESS, we use:

  • ESS Round 7; and
  • ESS Round 11.

Step 12: Import ESS Round 7

ess7 <- read_dta("../../data/ESS7e02_3.dta")

Inspect the country variable:

table(ess7$cntry)

Construct readable country names:

ess7 <- ess7 %>%
  mutate(
    country = countrycode(
      cntry,
      origin = "iso2c",
      destination = "country.name"
    )
  )

Step 13: Harmonise the health variable

The aim is to construct the health outcome in the same way in both rounds.

ess7 <- ess7 %>%
  mutate(
    health_good = case_when(
      health %in% c(1, 2) ~ 1,
      health %in% c(3, 4, 5) ~ 0,
      TRUE ~ NA_real_
    )
  )

Check the variable:

table(ess7$health_good, useNA = "ifany")

The coding is now identical to the indicator we constructed for ESS Round 11:

  • 1 = good or very good health;
  • 0 = fair, bad or very bad health.

This consistency is essential. If we changed the definition of the outcome between survey rounds, an apparent change over time could simply reflect a change in measurement.

Step 14: Harmonise education

We also construct the education groups in exactly the same way as for ESS Round 11.

ess7 <- ess7 %>%
  mutate(
    education = case_when(
      eisced %in% c(1, 2) ~ "Low",
      eisced %in% c(3, 4, 5) ~ "Medium",
      eisced %in% c(6, 7) ~ "High",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    ),
    education = factor(
      education,
      levels = c("Low", "Medium", "High")
    )
  )

Check the result:

table(ess7$education, useNA = "ifany")

We now have comparable measures of:

self-rated health × education

in both ESS rounds.

Step 16: Quantify change in the health gap

We can now calculate the absolute educational health gap separately for each survey round.

ch_gaps <- ch_time %>%
  filter(education %in% c("Low", "High")) %>%

    absolute_gap_pp = absolute_gap * 100,

Based on your results:

  2. Has the relative educational health inequality increased or decreased?

Instead of writing:

a more precise statement would be:

The absolute difference in good self-rated health between respondents with high and low education increased from X to Y percentage points between the two ESS rounds.

The second statement tells the reader what was measured, between whom, and using which measure of inequality.

Step 17: Extend the comparison across countries

We can now combine the two dimensions of comparison:

How do educational health inequalities differ across countries, and how have they changed over time?

Rather than examining Switzerland alone, repeat the calculation for several countries that participated in both ESS rounds.

Start by calculating health by country and education in ESS Round 7:

health_ess7 <- ess7 %>%
  filter(
    !is.na(country),
    !is.na(education)
  ) %>%
  group_by(country, education) %>%
  summarise(
    good_health = mean(health_good, na.rm = TRUE),
    n = sum(!is.na(health_good)),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  mutate(round = "ESS Round 7")

We already constructed the corresponding estimates for ESS Round 11:

health_ess11 <- health_by_country_education %>%
  mutate(round = "ESS Round 11")

Combine them:

health_over_time <- bind_rows(
  health_ess7,
  health_ess11
)

Select countries that occur in both rounds:

countries_both <- health_over_time %>%
  distinct(country, round) %>%
  count(country) %>%
  filter(n == 2) %>%
  pull(country)

Keep only these countries:

health_over_time <- health_over_time %>%
  filter(country %in% countries_both)
NoteWhy restrict the comparison?

The set of countries participating in the ESS is not identical in every round.

If we compared the average inequality across all countries in Round 7 with the average across all countries in Round 11, a difference could arise simply because the composition of countries changed.

Restricting the analysis to countries observed in both rounds makes the comparison more meaningful.

Step 18: Calculate country gaps in both rounds

We now calculate the low–high education gap for every country and round.

country_gaps_time <- health_over_time %>%
  filter(education %in% c("Low", "High")) %>%
  select(
    country,
    round,
    education,
    good_health
  ) %>%
  tidyr::pivot_wider(
    names_from = education,
    values_from = good_health
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    absolute_gap = High - Low,
    absolute_gap_pp = absolute_gap * 100,
    relative_ratio = High / Low
  )

Inspect the result:

country_gaps_time

We can now ask whether the educational health gap changed within each country.

country_gaps_time %>%
  select(
    country,
    round,
    absolute_gap_pp,
    relative_ratio
  ) %>%
  arrange(country, round)

For a smaller set of countries:

selected_countries <- c(
  "Switzerland",
  "Germany",
  "France",
  "Sweden"
)

country_gaps_time %>%
  filter(country %in% selected_countries)

A note on survey weights

So far, we have concentrated on the analytical logic of comparison.

Survey estimates can also be affected by the survey design and by differences in respondents’ probability of being included in the sample. Surveys therefore provide weights that can be used for particular types of analysis.

The ESS documentation provides detailed guidance on which weights should be used for different purposes. The choice becomes especially important when we move from estimating patterns within one country to comparing or combining countries.

NoteFor this session

Our main objective is to understand the logic of cross-national and over-time comparison, rather than to develop a complete survey-weighting strategy.

For your empirical project, however, you should check the documentation for the survey and waves you use and justify your choice of weights. We will discuss this when developing your individual analyses.

Part III: A second source of comparative evidence — the ISSP

The European Social Survey is not our only source of comparative data.

The International Social Survey Programme (ISSP) provides another opportunity to examine health and health inequality across countries and over time.

For this course, two datasets are particularly useful:

  • the ISSP Health and Health Care 2011 module; and
  • the ISSP Health and Health Care II 2021 module.

Because both modules focus explicitly on health, they contain measures that are not available in every ESS round.

Import the datasets:

issp11 <- read_dta("../../data/ZA5800_v3-0-0.dta")
issp21 <- read_dta("../../data/ZA8000_v2-0-0.dta")

Inspect them:

dim(issp11)

ESS and ISSP are complementary — not interchangeable

The ESS and ISSP allow us to examine similar substantive questions, but they should not simply be pooled together.

Differences may exist in:

  • questionnaire wording;
  • response categories;
  • education classifications;
  • survey years;
  • participating countries;
  • sampling procedures; and
  • weighting.

If an inequality estimate differs between the ESS and ISSP, we therefore cannot immediately conclude that health inequality itself changed.

The difference may partly reflect measurement or survey design.

Comparability is an empirical assumption, not a property that comes automatically with having two datasets.

Before comparing estimates, ask whether the underlying concepts, measurements, populations and survey procedures are sufficiently comparable for the question you want to answer.

From description to explanation

At this point, we can describe substantial variation.

We can ask:

  • Which countries have better average health?
  • Which countries have larger educational health inequalities?
  • Are inequalities larger in absolute or relative terms?
  • Have inequalities changed over time?

But there is an important limit to what we have done.

Suppose we observe that educational health inequality is larger in Country A than in Country B.

That observation does not tell us why.

Possible explanations might involve:

  • differences in the composition of the population;
  • labour-market conditions;
  • income inequality;
  • healthcare institutions;
  • social policies;
  • health behaviours;
  • material living conditions;
  • psychosocial factors; or
  • other contextual differences.

Distinguishing these explanations requires a different analytical step.

Description tells us where inequalities exist. Explanation asks why they exist.

We will return to explanations in Session 5.

Before doing so, however, Session 4 introduces another important question: how people themselves perceive and explain health inequalities.

Your empirical project

You now have the basic tools needed to formulate a genuinely comparative research question.

A useful structure is:

How does [health outcome] vary by [dimension of inequality] across [countries and/or time]?

Examples include:

How does educational inequality in self-rated health vary across European countries?

Did educational inequality in self-rated health change between two ESS rounds?

Is the gender gap in self-rated health similar across countries?

Does the association between subjective social position and health vary across countries?

For your own project, start thinking about four choices:

  1. Health outcome: What aspect of health are you interested in?
  2. Dimension of inequality: Between which groups or positions will you compare health?
  3. Comparative dimension: Which countries and/or survey waves will you compare?
  4. Measure: Will you use an absolute measure, a relative measure, or both?

Recap

In Sessions 2 and 3, we have moved from the concept of health inequality to comparative empirical analysis.

You should now be able to distinguish between three questions:

How healthy is a population?

How unequally is health distributed within that population?

How do those inequalities vary across countries and over time?

Several principles are worth retaining:

  1. Health levels and health inequalities are different quantities. A healthier population is not necessarily a more equal population.
  2. Comparison provides a benchmark. A health gap becomes more informative when we can compare it across countries or over time.
  3. The measure matters. Absolute and relative inequalities can develop differently.
  4. Comparability must be examined. Similar variable labels do not necessarily imply identical measurement.
  5. Description is not explanation. Cross-national variation can generate hypotheses about why inequalities differ, but it does not by itself establish the causes.

The empirical sequence so far has therefore been:

Observe → measure → compare

Next session: How do people perceive and explain health inequalities?

So far, we as researchers have defined and measured health inequality.

In Session 4, we change perspective.

People also form their own judgements about why some groups are healthier than others. They may attribute health differences to, for example, individual choices and behaviours or to social and structural circumstances.

These explanations may matter for whether people regard health inequalities as a problem and whether they support policies intended to address them.

The next session therefore asks:

How do people perceive and explain health inequalities?

We will examine public perceptions and causal attributions of health inequality and consider how these relate to judgements about inequality and public policy.

This also introduces an important distinction that will remain with us throughout the course:

Observing an inequality, explaining its causes, judging whether it is unfair, and deciding what policy should do about it are four different questions.
